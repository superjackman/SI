Training in practice

Mini-batch SGD, Adam, regularisation, validation set

▶ Open the interactive version (diagrams, labs, quizzes)

The problem Train fast, train stably, and still generalise.

Why care

Millions of samples: do not scan all every step; also avoid memorising.

Everyday analogy

  • Mini-batches estimate the slope cheaply.
  • Adam gives each knob its own step size.
  • Regularisation / early stopping fight overfitting.

Core idea

  • Mini-batch SGD: estimate the gradient from a small random batch (say 256). Much cheaper; the noise can even help escape bad regions.
  • Momentum accumulates past gradients to damp the zig-zag; Adam (Kingma and Ba, 2014) gives each parameter its own adaptive step. Large models use the AdamW variant with learning-rate warm-up and decay.
  • Overfitting: training loss keeps falling while new data gets worse. Regularisation: L2, dropout, data augmentation, early stopping.
  • Data splits: train to fit the parameters, validation to pick hyper-parameters and when to stop, test once at the very end.

What the picture shows

  • Sample a batch → gradient → Adam → check val.

The numbers

  • 1M / 256 ≈ 3900 updates per epoch; momentum .
  • 1 million examples, batch 256: about 3,900 updates per pass; full-batch gradient descent makes 1.
  • Momentum (same as card KM13): momentum 0.9, slope keeps the same sign 1, 1, 1, 1 → , faster and faster.
  • Train 99%, validation 70%: overfitting — regularise, add data or shrink the model. Both 60%: underfitting.

Formula

  • v = βv + g; Adam update
  • Momentum: v = β × v + g, w = w − η × v
  • Adam: m = β1 × m + (1 − β1) × g, v = β2 × v + (1 − β2) × g², w = w − η × m̂ / (√v̂ + ε)
  • L2: L′ = L + λ × Σ w²
